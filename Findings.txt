Findings
===============================================

Five passwords of varying complexity were created and tested using the "How Secure Is My Password?" online tool.
Results included crack time estimation and visual feedback indicating the relative strength of each password.

Passwords Tested:
-----------------

1. 12345678
   - Crack Time: Instantly
   - Feedback: Extremely weak, common password. Vulnerable to instant brute-force/dictionary attacks.
   - Screenshot: image.jpg (1st)

2. Password
   - Crack Time: Instantly
   - Feedback: Weak, based on dictionary word and commonly used password.
   - Screenshot: image.jpg (2nd)

3. Elevate_Cyber@123
   - Crack Time: 3 hundred quadrillion years
   - Feedback: Strong, contains uppercase, lowercase, numbers, symbols, and good length.
   - Screenshot: image.jpg (3rd)

4. Pass89236
   - Crack Time: 3 days
   - Feedback: Better than basic, includes numbers, but pattern and length still make it crackable in a few days.
   - Screenshot: image.jpg (4th)

5. Br!ll!@nt
   - Crack Time: 17 years
   - Feedback: Medium-strong; uses symbols and varied capitalization, but could be further improved by lengthening and avoiding dictionary roots.
   - Screenshot: image.jpg (5th)

Key Observations:
-----------------
- Very short/simple/common passwords can be cracked instantly by automated attacks.
- Adding length, numbers, mixed case, and especially symbols increases crack time exponentially.
- Uncommon words or passphrases with random elements performed best.
- “Password” and numeric patterns (`12345678`) were the weakest and flagged as dangerous by the tool.
- Even some 8-10 character passwords are no longer sufficiently “strong” if they use dictionary roots or simple patterns.

Best Practices (Based on Results):
----------------------------------
- Use long passwords (12+ characters where possible)
- Mix uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols
- Avoid dictionary words, predictable patterns, or repeat use
- Consider passphrases or random word combinations for high security
- Use password managers for truly random, unique passwords per account

End of findings.
